# Offline Crop and Disease Care Guide

This manual covers all 38 labels in the bundled model. The demo displays the suggested method for the top-ranked label. Healthy classes receive routine care and monitoring guidance rather than disease treatment.

**Important:** a photo prediction is not a confirmed diagnosis. These general, source-linked steps do not guarantee a cure and may not fit every location or growing system. Confirm uncertain or serious cases with local agricultural extension or a qualified agronomist. Do not use crop-protection products based only on a model result; follow local registration rules and product-label directions.

## Apple — Apple scab

Possible apple scab, a fungal disease that can cause olive-green to dark leaf spots and scabby or cracked fruit. A photo-based label is not a confirmed diagnosis; similar symptoms can have other causes.

**One suggested method**
After leaf fall, clear infected fallen leaves from beneath the tree and dispose of them according to local guidance; this reduces overwintering disease material.

### Other steps

- Check several leaves and any fruit for olive-green to dark, slightly raised or velvety spots; note whether leaves yellow or drop early.
- Rake up fallen affected leaves and remove fallen diseased fruit; dispose of them according to local guidance, or shred fallen leaves to help them break down.
- Avoid wetting foliage when watering; if practical, prune during the appropriate dormant season to improve airflow and leaf drying.
- Contact your local extension service or an orchard agronomist soon if symptoms are widespread, substantial leaf drop or fruit damage occurs, the problem returns in successive seasons, or identification is uncertain.

### Prevention and follow-up

- Clean up fallen leaves after leaf drop each year; the fungus can persist in leaf litter.
- Maintain an open, uncrowded canopy and use watering practices that let foliage dry promptly.
- When planting or replacing a tree, choose a scab-resistant variety suitable for your area; resistance can vary by location and may not be complete.

**Caution:** These steps can reduce disease pressure but do not confirm the photo diagnosis or cure existing lesions. If considering chemical control, use only a product registered for the crop and site in your location, exactly as its label directs and with local extension advice; timing and permitted uses vary.

## Apple — Black rot

Black rot is a fungal disease that can affect apple fruit, leaves, and branches. It may cause brown, sometimes ringed fruit rot, purple-edged ‘frog-eye’ leaf spots, or sunken reddish-brown branch cankers. A photo-based label is only a clue, not a confirmed diagnosis.

**One suggested method**
Remove and discard mummified fruit and clearly dead or infected wood; do not leave it under the tree.

### Other steps

- Check the tree and nearby apples for shriveled fruit left on branches, dead or diseased limbs, and sunken or cracked cankers.
- Remove and discard mummified fruit and infected or dead plant material; do not leave it beneath the tree or compost it on site.
- Prune out dead or diseased branches and cankers in dry conditions; avoid removing large limbs or cutting near the trunk if you are unsure how to prune safely.
- Contact your local extension service or a qualified tree-fruit specialist promptly if cankers or dieback affect major limbs or the trunk, the problem is spreading quickly, or you need confirmation before taking substantial action.

### Prevention and follow-up

- Keep fallen and attached diseased fruit and pruned infected wood cleaned up; maintain good airflow with appropriate pruning.
- Reduce tree stress by providing adequate water and choosing apple varieties suited to your local climate; avoid prolonged leaf wetness where practical.
- Monitor fruit, leaves, and branches during the growing season for new spots, rot, or cankers.

**Caution:** A single image cannot reliably confirm black rot; other problems can look similar, so seek local extension or agronomist confirmation when symptoms are uncertain or serious. If chemical control is considered, use only a product registered for apples and black rot in your location, exactly according to its label and local extension advice; this guidance does not imply a cure.

## Apple — Cedar apple rust

Cedar apple rust is a fungal disease involving both apple-family trees and junipers or red cedars. It can cause orange-red leaf spots and, in some cases, fruit lesions or premature leaf loss. A photo-based identification is provisional: similar rust diseases can look alike, so confirm the diagnosis if symptoms are unclear or consequential.

**One suggested method**
Where feasible, remove rust galls from nearby junipers or red cedars in late winter before orange spore structures appear; ask local extension first if unsure.

### Other steps

- Check both leaf surfaces for orange-red spots and small tube-like growths beneath them; photograph the whole tree and close-ups for comparison or local diagnosis.
- If infected twigs or branches are present, prune and remove them. In late winter or early spring, inspect nearby junipers/red cedars and remove rust galls before their orange, gelatinous spore structures appear, where feasible.
- Water at soil level rather than wetting foliage, and maintain sensible canopy airflow to reduce leaf-wetness time.
- If symptoms are widespread or identification is uncertain, send photos or a sample to your local university extension or plant diagnostic service before taking major action.

### Prevention and follow-up

- When planting or replacing an apple tree, choose a locally adapted cedar-apple-rust-resistant variety; check with local extension because resistance varies by cultivar and region.
- Avoid planting susceptible apples close to junipers or red cedars where feasible, and inspect nearby alternate hosts in late winter; distance and gall removal can reduce risk but may not prevent infection because spores travel far.

**Caution:** Limited leaf spotting often does not require urgent intervention. Seek local extension or an agronomist promptly if the tree has substantial or repeated premature leaf loss, significant fruit damage, or rapidly worsening symptoms. Do not remove nearby trees wholesale based only on this photo diagnosis; rust identity and severity need confirmation, and removing nearby junipers may not eliminate risk.

## Cherry (including sour) — Powdery mildew

Possible powdery mildew on cherry: it often appears as small pale or white powdery patches on young leaves, commonly first on leaf undersides or sucker shoots; leaves may later curl or distort. A photo label is not a confirmed diagnosis—other problems can look similar, so have a local extension service or qualified plant-health professional confirm it, especially if symptoms are spreading or fruit is affected.

**One suggested method**
If powdery growth is confirmed on sucker shoots, remove and discard those shoots; avoid moving the material to other cherry trees.

### Other steps

- Check several young leaves, especially their undersides and leaves on root suckers, for white powdery patches; take clear close-up photos and note whether symptoms are spreading.
- If powdery growth is present on sucker shoots, remove those shoots and dispose of them away from the tree; avoid moving potentially infected plant material to other cherry trees.
- Avoid overhead irrigation that wets leaves or developing fruit. If infection seems to be spreading, fruit is affected, or the tree is in a commercial planting, contact local extension or an agronomist promptly for confirmation and locally appropriate advice.
- If symptoms are severe, uncertain, or continue appearing on new growth, seek local diagnosis rather than relying on the image label alone.

### Prevention and follow-up

- Monitor young leaves, suckers, and lower/interior canopy regularly, particularly after spring irrigation and during warm, humid periods.
- Maintain an open canopy with appropriate pruning to improve airflow; avoid excessive nitrogen or overly vigorous growth.
- When planting or replacing trees, ask local extension about cherry cultivars with resistance or lower susceptibility in your area; susceptibility differs by cultivar and region.

**Caution:** Image-based identification is provisional, and these steps may reduce favorable conditions but do not guarantee control or cure. Powdery mildew can spread rapidly under favorable conditions, and regional guidance differs. Do not apply pesticides based on this result; if chemical control is considered, use only a locally registered product exactly according to its label and local extension advice.

## Corn (maize) — Cercospora leaf spot Gray leaf spot

Gray leaf spot is a fungal disease of corn. It often begins on lower leaves as tan-to-gray, long rectangular lesions whose edges follow leaf veins; lesions can merge and kill leaf tissue. A photo-based result is only a clue, since other corn leaf problems can look similar.

**One suggested method**
For future crops, rotate away from corn and manage infected corn residue after harvest where practical; seek local advice if the disease is spreading in this crop.

### Other steps

- Check several plants, especially lower leaves, for rectangular tan or gray lesions bounded by veins; note whether lesions are moving upward or appearing before tasseling.
- If irrigating, avoid keeping foliage wet for long periods; where practical, water early enough for leaves to dry and avoid unnecessary overhead watering.
- For a small planting, remove badly affected leaves only if practical, and dispose of diseased debris rather than leaving it near healthy plants. For field-scale disease, do not attempt broad leaf removal; seek local advice.
- Contact a local university extension office or crop adviser promptly if lesions are spreading upward, appear before tasseling, or affect many plants; ask them to confirm the diagnosis and assess field risk.

### Prevention and follow-up

- After harvest, manage infected corn residue where practical; the fungus can survive in corn debris, and rotation away from corn and residue burial can reduce risk.
- For future plantings, choose corn hybrids with better local gray-leaf-spot tolerance and rotate away from corn when feasible.
- Scout regularly during the season, especially from tasseling onward, and keep notes or photos to track whether symptoms are progressing.

**Caution:** A single leaf photo cannot confirm gray leaf spot, and similar-looking diseases can require different responses. These steps reduce risk; they do not promise a cure. Get local extension or agronomist advice promptly for fast-spreading or upper-canopy disease, especially before tasseling. Do not apply a pesticide based only on this image; if chemical control is considered, use only a locally registered product exactly according to its label and local extension advice.

## Corn (maize) — Common rust

Common rust is a fungal disease of corn that can cause raised, reddish-brown elongated spots on both sides of leaves; spots may darken with age. It often has little effect on commercial hybrids, but severe infection can yellow or kill leaf tissue. A photo-only diagnosis needs confirmation: southern rust and other leaf problems can look similar, and a local extension specialist or agronomist can check the symptoms.

**One suggested method**
Common rust often needs no treatment; monitor the crop and ask a local crop adviser about a resistant hybrid for the next planting if it has been severe.

### Other steps

- Check several plants and both sides of leaves for raised, elongated rust-colored spots; take clear close-up photos and note whether symptoms are spreading.
- Avoid prolonged leaf wetness where practical: water at the soil rather than over the leaves, and avoid unnecessary handling of wet plants.
- Monitor affected plants and nearby corn. Do not remove plants or treat based only on this image; ask local extension or an agronomist promptly if infection is widespread, worsening quickly, leaves are dying, or plants are near tasseling or silking.
- If confirmation matters for a crop decision, share photos or a fresh sample with local extension or an agronomist, especially if the spots are orange and mostly on upper leaf surfaces or otherwise do not match the description.

### Prevention and follow-up

- For future planting, choose a locally adapted hybrid or variety with common-rust resistance when available.
- Scout periodically during cool, humid or dewy weather. Rotation and removal of old corn debris are not dependable controls because rust spores can arrive on the wind and the fungus does not generally persist in corn debris; avoid relying on them as a cure.

**Caution:** A photo cannot reliably distinguish common rust from southern rust or other causes. Common rust often has limited impact, but widespread or fast-progressing symptoms merit timely local expert review. No pesticide is recommended here; any chemical control should be considered only with local extension advice and, if used, a product registered locally for the crop and disease exactly according to its label.

## Corn (maize) — Northern Leaf Blight

Northern corn leaf blight is a fungal disease that can cause long, gray-green to tan, cigar-shaped leaf lesions and extensive leaf blighting. A photo-based label is only a clue: other diseases or stress can look similar, so confirm by checking multiple plants and consulting local Extension or a plant diagnostic service if symptoms are uncertain or worsening.

**One suggested method**
Choose a locally adapted hybrid with resistance to northern corn leaf blight for future plantings; consult local extension if current symptoms are spreading rapidly.

### Other steps

- Inspect several plants, including lower and upper leaves, for expanding cigar-shaped lesions; note how quickly they are spreading and the crop growth stage.
- Limit prolonged leaf wetness: if irrigating, avoid keeping foliage wet for long periods; where possible, water at midday rather than in the evening or overnight.
- If lesions are spreading rapidly, affect upper leaves, or appear before or around tasseling, contact your local Extension office or an agronomist promptly for field-specific assessment, especially before considering any treatment.
- After harvest, manage infected corn residue to encourage decomposition where appropriate; avoid moving infected debris to clean fields.

### Prevention and follow-up

- For future plantings, choose hybrids with locally reliable resistance to northern corn leaf blight, with advice from local Extension or a trusted seed adviser.
- Rotate away from corn with a non-host crop for multiple seasons where practical; rotation can reduce residue-borne inoculum, but windborne spores may still arrive from nearby fields.
- Scout fields regularly, especially during warm, humid or prolonged-wet periods, so disease is noticed early.

**Caution:** A single plant photo cannot confirm northern corn leaf blight, and related diseases or abiotic injury may look similar. Seek local help promptly for fast-spreading symptoms or disease present before/around tasseling, when yield impact can be greater. No pesticide is prescribed here; use a chemical product only if it is locally registered for this crop and disease, exactly according to its label and local Extension advice.

## Grape — Black rot

Possible grape black rot, a fungal disease that can affect leaves, shoots, and fruit. Typical signs include tan-to-brown leaf spots with dark margins and tiny black dots, or grapes that turn brown, then shrivel into hard black mummies. A photo-based label is not a confirmed diagnosis; other problems can look similar.

**One suggested method**
Remove and destroy mummified berries and infected canes or fallen debris so they do not remain beneath or on the vines.

### Other steps

- Check several leaves and fruit clusters for matching signs, and take clear photos of the whole vine and close-ups; avoid relying on one spot alone.
- Remove visibly infected berries or clusters, fallen mummies, and infected canes when practical. Bag and discard or otherwise destroy them; do not leave mummified fruit under or hanging on the vine.
- Keep foliage and clusters from staying wet for long periods: water at soil level when possible, and carefully thin dense growth to improve airflow.
- Contact a local extension office or grape-growing agronomist promptly if many clusters are affected, symptoms are spreading during wet weather, or the diagnosis is uncertain; ask for advice before the next growing season if this has recurred.

### Prevention and follow-up

- Before new growth begins, remove and destroy mummified berries and infected plant material; clear fallen leaves and fruit from around vines.
- Maintain an open canopy with appropriate pruning and shoot or leaf thinning to improve airflow and reduce humid, slow-drying conditions.
- When planting or replacing vines, ask local extension about varieties less susceptible in your area; no variety is completely resistant.

**Caution:** This is general guidance, not a diagnosis or a guarantee of control. A photo alone cannot confirm black rot, and similar symptoms may have other causes. Black rot can cause serious fruit loss, so seek local extension or agronomist guidance promptly if it appears widespread or is affecting fruit. Chemical control is not specified here; if considered, use only a locally registered product exactly according to its label and local extension advice.

## Grape — Esca (Black Measles)

Esca (black measles) is a grapevine trunk disease that can cause striped or scorched leaves, spotted berries, wood decay, and sometimes sudden vine collapse. These symptoms overlap with other grape problems, and they may not appear every year; a photo-based identification is only a possibility and needs confirmation. Infected wood is not cured by fungicide sprays.

**One suggested method**
There is no established curative spray for Esca; have the vine confirmed locally and prune only in dry conditions, removing dead wood as advised.

### Other steps

- Mark and photograph affected vines and ask your local extension service or a grape agronomist to confirm the diagnosis, especially if symptoms recur or the vine is declining.
- Avoid pruning during or just before wet weather; make necessary cuts in dry conditions and avoid large cuts where practical.
- Remove pruned or clearly dead/diseased wood from the vineyard, and clean pruning tools between vines.
- Seek prompt local advice if a vine suddenly wilts or collapses, or if several vines are affected; ask whether trunk renewal or vine removal is appropriate rather than removing vines based on a photo alone.

### Prevention and follow-up

- Start with healthy, reputable planting material; keep tools and propagation equipment clean.
- Reduce vine stress with locally appropriate irrigation, drainage, and fertility practices, particularly for young vines.
- Limit pruning wounds where practical and ask local extension about region-appropriate wound protection and pruning timing.

**Caution:** Esca is a complex trunk disease, and similar leaf, berry, or decline symptoms can have other causes. A photo cannot confirm it, and suspected infection is not a reason by itself to remove a vine. There is no established curative spray for infected wood. If considering any chemical product, use only one registered for the intended use in your location, exactly according to its label and local extension advice.

## Grape — Leaf blight (Isariopsis Leaf Spot)

### Prevention and follow-up

- Maintain an open canopy through appropriate pruning and shoot positioning, and inspect vines regularly during the growing season.
- Where replanting, ask local extension about grape varieties less susceptible to diseases in your region.

**Caution:** A leaf photo cannot reliably confirm Isariopsis leaf spot or rule out look-alike diseases and other causes. Avoid treating from the image label alone; ask local extension or an agronomist to confirm, especially before any chemical treatment. If a chemical is considered, use only a locally registered product exactly according to its label and local extension advice.

## Tomato — Leaf Mold

Tomato leaf mold is a fungal disease (Passalora fulva) that commonly affects tomatoes in humid greenhouses and high tunnels. It often starts on older leaves as pale yellow spots on top with velvety olive-to-brown growth underneath. A photo-based label is only a possibility, not a confirmed diagnosis.

**One suggested method**
Remove and bag affected leaves, and improve airflow around the plant; confirm locally if symptoms are spreading.

### Other steps

- Check both sides of older leaves: leaf mold often has pale yellow spots above and velvety olive-brown growth directly underneath. Similar-looking problems can have other causes.
- If symptoms fit, remove and bag affected leaves. In a greenhouse or high tunnel where leaf mold is present, remove affected plants and nearby plants; discard infected material away from tomato crops.
- Clean tools after handling affected plants. Water at soil level rather than over the foliage, and improve spacing and airflow where practical.
- Contact your local agricultural extension service or an agronomist promptly if the diagnosis is uncertain, several plants are affected or symptoms are spreading, or leaves, flowers, or fruit are being lost.

### Prevention and follow-up

- Reduce prolonged leaf wetness and humidity: avoid overhead watering, space and stake plants for airflow, and ventilate greenhouses or high tunnels.
- Remove crop debris after the season and clean tools and reusable supports; rotate tomatoes to a different growing area when practical.
- Consider locally recommended resistant varieties, recognizing that resistance may not protect against every pathogen strain.

**Caution:** A single photo cannot confirm leaf mold; nutrient problems and other diseases may look similar. Look for the characteristic growth on the underside of spotted leaves and seek local confirmation before removing many plants. No pesticide is prescribed here.

## Tomato — Septoria leaf spot

Possible Septoria leaf spot on tomato. It often begins on lower leaves as many small spots with pale centers and dark edges; severe infection can yellow and drop leaves. A photo-based label is only a screening clue, not a confirmed diagnosis.

**One suggested method**
Remove a few affected leaves and discard them rather than composting heavily infected material; keep foliage dry.

### Other steps

- If only a few leaves are affected, remove them and discard them in household waste or destroy them where permitted; do not compost heavily infected material.
- Water at soil level, preferably early in the day, and avoid splashing water onto leaves.

- Contact a local extension office or qualified agronomist promptly if spots are spreading quickly, many plants are affected, or substantial leaf drop is occurring; ask for diagnosis if uncertain.

- At season’s end, remove diseased tomato debris and nearby host weeds; use clean seed or transplants.
- Where practical, rotate tomatoes away from tomato, potato, and other related crops for about 3 years, and mulch soil to reduce splash.
- Choose locally adapted tomato varieties with reported tolerance where available; tolerance is not complete resistance.

**Caution:** Leaf spots can resemble other diseases, including early blight, and a single photo may not show diagnostic details. Treat this as a preliminary possibility; seek local confirmation if the diagnosis is uncertain or the disease is worsening.

## Tomato — Spider mites Two-spotted spider mite

Possible two-spotted spider mite damage on tomato. These tiny mites feed on leaf undersides and can cause fine pale speckles, bronzing, and sometimes webbing, especially in hot, dry conditions. A photo-based label is not a confirmed diagnosis; check the leaf undersides or shake a leaf over white paper to look for moving mites.

**One suggested method**
If mites are present, wash the undersides of leaves with a firm stream of water while avoiding damage to the plant.

### Other steps

- Inspect the undersides of speckled leaves with a hand lens, or tap a leaf over white paper and look for tiny moving dots.
- If mites are present, wash leaf undersides with a firm stream of water to dislodge mites and webbing; avoid damaging the plant.
- Keep the tomato adequately watered, without waterlogging it, and remove and discard severely infested leaves if practical.
- Contact local extension or an agronomist promptly if damage is spreading quickly, webbing or leaf drop is extensive, or you cannot confirm the cause.

### Prevention and follow-up

- Check leaf undersides every few days, especially during hot, dry weather and after bringing new plants into the growing area.
- Reduce plant stress with steady watering and mulch; avoid dusty, dry conditions and excessive nitrogen fertilizer.
- Conserve beneficial predators by avoiding unnecessary broad-spectrum insecticides.

**Caution:** Leaf speckling and yellowing can have other causes, so confirm mites before taking control action. If an intervention beyond water and sanitation is considered, ask local extension for advice and use only a locally registered product exactly as its label directs; this guidance does not promise a cure.

## Tomato — Target Spot

Possible tomato target spot, a fungal disease caused by Corynespora cassiicola. It can cause brown leaf spots that develop pale centers and dark concentric rings, but symptoms can resemble early blight or bacterial spot. A photo-based identification is not confirmation.

**One suggested method**
Remove badly affected leaves and spotted fruit, discard them away from the garden, and improve airflow around the plant.

## Tomato — Tomato Yellow Leaf Curl Virus

Tomato yellow leaf curl virus (TYLCV) is a serious tomato disease spread mainly by whiteflies. Infected plants may have curled, crumpled yellow young leaves, stunted or bushy growth, and flower drop. A photo-based label is only a clue, not a confirmed diagnosis: nutrient problems, heat or water stress, and other diseases can look similar.

**One suggested method**
There is no cure for TYLCV; get local confirmation promptly, and remove/contain affected plants only if infection is confirmed or local extension advises it.

### Other steps

- Contact your local extension office, plant clinic, or agronomist promptly for confirmation and local advice, especially if plants are young or several plants are affected; symptoms can take weeks to appear.
- If infection is confirmed or strongly suspected and only a few plants are affected, remove and contain them, then dispose of them as local guidance allows; do not compost them. Remove volunteer tomato plants and crop remains.
- Check leaf undersides for whiteflies and monitor with sticky cards. Protect healthy plants with fine-mesh covers or reflective mulch where suitable, and ask local extension for locally appropriate vector-control options.
- Keep watering even and avoid plant stress. This supports plant health but does not cure TYLCV or stop whiteflies from spreading it.

### Prevention and follow-up

- Start with healthy, locally appropriate transplants and tomato varieties listed as TYLCV-resistant or tolerant; tolerance is not immunity.
- Rotate with non-host crops where practical, and remove tomato volunteers and old crop residue between plantings.
- Monitor whiteflies early and use locally recommended prevention. Any pesticide, if advised, must be registered for the crop and location and used exactly according to its label and local extension advice.

**Caution:** Do not treat the image diagnosis as definitive. Similar leaf symptoms can have other causes, so seek local confirmation. TYLCV can spread quickly through whiteflies and infected plants; get local extension or agronomist advice promptly. Removing a plant is most useful when infection is confirmed or strongly suspected and incidence is low; follow local plant-waste guidance. No measure described here cures an already infected plant.

## Tomato — Tomato mosaic virus

This label means the photo model suspects tomato mosaic virus (ToMV) on tomato; it is not a confirmed diagnosis. Leaf mottling, curling, or distortion can have other causes, and plant viruses require lab testing for confirmation. There is no cure, so focus on preventing spread.

**One suggested method**
There is no cure for tomato mosaic virus; wash hands and clean tools between plants, and seek lab or extension confirmation before removing plants.

### Other steps

- If mosaic-like symptoms are present, keep the plant separate and avoid touching healthy plants after it; wash hands and clean tools between plants.
- Contact your local agricultural extension service or plant-diagnostic lab promptly to confirm the cause, especially if more than one plant is affected or the crop is valuable.
- If ToMV is confirmed, or local extension advises removal, pull affected plants and fallen debris, bag and discard them rather than composting; clean tools and any equipment that contacted them.

### Prevention and follow-up

- Use disease-free seed and transplants, and choose tomato varieties with resistance to ToMV where available; resistance is not complete protection against all viruses.
- Use good handling sanitation, avoid tobacco products around plants, and wash hands after tobacco use and before gardening. ToMV mainly spreads through contaminated hands, tools, seed, and debris—not insect vectors, so insect spraying is not a ToMV control.
- Remove volunteer tomatoes and relevant infected plant debris; ask local extension whether rotation is useful for your site, since ToMV has a broad host range and rotation alone may not prevent it.

**Caution:** A photograph cannot confirm ToMV, and other viruses, herbicide injury, or environmental problems can look similar. Seek local extension or diagnostic-lab advice soon for confirmation and site-specific action; seek help urgently if symptoms are appearing on multiple plants or spreading. No pesticide cures a virus. If any pesticide is considered for a separately identified pest, use only a locally registered product exactly according to its label and local extension advice.
